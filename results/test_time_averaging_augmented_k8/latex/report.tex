       \large Three sweeps, $k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$, ogbg-molpcba (81 runs)}
\author{ogbg-molpcba experiments, GIN backbone}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We compare three ways of resolving the sign + basis ambiguity of Laplacian
eigenvector positional encodings (LapPE) on the ogbg-molpcba molecular
benchmark: (i) deterministic canonicalization (\texttt{map}, \texttt{oap}),
(ii) training-time random augmentation (\texttt{random\_augmented}), and
(iii) random augmentation \emph{plus} test-time averaging over $K$ random
draws of the eigenvector ambiguity group. Three sweeps (81 runs total) at
$k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$ eigenvectors, hidden dim
$h\!\in\!\{16,128,512\}$, three seeds each.
\textbf{Bottom line: the verdict is non-monotonic in $k$.} Random
augmentation is the best choice around \mbox{$k=8$} (wins all $h$); at
\mbox{$k=3$} it wins at small/medium $h$ but loses to \texttt{oap} at
\mbox{$h=512$}; at \mbox{$k=16$}, \texttt{oap} wins at every $h$. Test-time
averaging adds a tiny consistent bump that grows with $k$ ($+0.0001$ at
\mbox{$k\!=\!3$} to $+0.0008$ at \mbox{$k\!=\!16$}), never hurts, and is
always too small to flip a verdict. The practical recommendation is
therefore \emph{$k$-dependent}: use \texttt{random\_augmented} around
\mbox{$k=8$}; use \texttt{oap} otherwise.
\end{abstract}

%==============================================================================
\section{What problem are we solving?}
%==============================================================================

Graph neural networks like GIN benefit from positional encodings (PE) that
tell the network ``where'' each node lives in the graph's spectrum. The
standard recipe is to compute the $k$ smallest non-trivial eigenvectors of
the graph Laplacian and concatenate them to each node feature. This works,
but eigenvectors are not unique: for any simple eigenvalue, the eigenvector
is defined only up to a sign $(\pm 1)$; for a multiplicity-$m$ eigenvalue,
\emph{any orthogonal change of basis $Q\in O(m)$} produces an equally valid
eigenbasis. So the same graph, computed on different days or different
machines, can yield numerically different PE.

Three ways to handle this ambiguity:

\paragraph{Canonicalization.} Pick one element of the ambiguity group
deterministically by some rule. Examples:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \texttt{map} --- maximum-absolute-position rule per column.
  \item \texttt{oap} --- ordered-absolute-position rule, more robust at
        multiplicity blocks.
\end{itemize}
The model sees the \emph{same} PE every epoch and at test time.

\paragraph{Random augmentation.}
(\texttt{random\_augmented}.) Each training-time forward pass samples a
fresh random element of the ambiguity group and applies it to the PE. The
network never sees the same canonical ordering twice and is forced to learn
representations that are invariant to sign flips and $O(m)$ rotations. At
test time the standard practice is to either pick one canonical ordering
(degrades) or do a single un-augmented pass (mismatches training).

\paragraph{Random augmentation $+$ test-time averaging.}
(\texttt{random\_augmented} with $K$-sample averaging at test time.) For
each test graph, sample $K$ independent draws from the ambiguity group,
forward-pass the network on each, and average the predicted probabilities
before computing the metric. This is the test-time analog of training-time
augmentation --- you take an empirical Monte-Carlo estimate of the
\emph{group-invariant} prediction. Inspired by the
\texttt{test\_with\_randomized\_invariance} helper in
\href{https://github.com/jwsiegel2510/Sn-invariant-weighted-frames}%
{Siegel \emph{et al.}, weighted-frames}; we adapt it from $S_n$ to the
LapPE ambiguity group $\prod_i \mathrm{O}(m_i)$.

The augmentation rng is seeded as
$(\text{base\_seed} \cdot 1{,}000{,}003 + s) \times 2{,}654{,}435{,}761

for a given $(\text{seed}, K)$.

\section{Interpretation}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{The verdict is non-monotonic in $k$}

Across the $81$ runs, \texttt{random\_augmented} is the best method in
$5$ of $9$ $(k,h)$ cells and \texttt{oap} in $4$. The split is not random
in $(k,h)$ space --- it tracks $k$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item At $k\!=\!3$, \texttt{random\_augmented} wins $2$ of $3$
      ($h\!=\!16,128$) and \texttt{oap} narrowly wins $h\!=\!512$.
\item At $k\!=\!8$, \texttt{random\_augmented} wins everywhere.
\item At $k\!=\!16$, \texttt{oap} wins everywhere.
\end{itemize}

This is genuinely non-monotonic: random augmentation's edge appears,
peaks, and disappears as the PE width grows. The $k\!=\!8$
\texttt{random\_augmented} sweep is not the start of an ever-improving
trend --- it is an isolated sweet spot.

\paragraph{Recommendation.} \emph{$k$-dependent:} use
\texttt{random\_augmented} around $k\!=\!8$, where it dominates every
$h$. Outside that --- \mbox{$k=3$ at large $h$}, or $k\!=\!16$
anywhere --- \texttt{oap} is at least as good and often better. There is
no single training method that wins across the entire $(k,h)$ grid.

\subsection{Why does $k\!=\!8$ favour augmentation but $k\!=\!16$ does not?}

The simplest hypothesis that fits all three sweeps: random augmentation
trades off two effects against each other as $k$ grows.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item \emph{Information.} A wider PE carries more structural signal.
      Canonicalization compresses that signal into a deterministic
      ordering and just hands it to the network. So
      $\Delta\text{AP}_\text{oap}$ is roughly monotonically non-decreasing
      in $k$ (Table~\ref{tab:compare}: at $h=512$ \texttt{oap} goes
      $0.2431\!\to\!0.2371\!\to\!0.2397$ across $k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$ ---
      flat to slightly increasing).
\item \emph{Invariance budget.} \texttt{random\_augmented} forces the
      network to learn invariance over the full ambiguity group
      $\mathbb{Z}_2^k$ (size $2^k$). At $k\!=\!3$ that's $8$ patterns ---
      easy. At $k\!=\!8$, $256$ patterns --- still well within reach
      after $\sim$$80$ epochs. At $k\!=\!16$, $65{,}536$ patterns --- the
      $200$-epoch budget is no longer enough; the network under-fits the
      invariance and degrades.
\end{itemize}

The $k\!=\!8$ sweet spot is the $k$ where the wider PE has paid off but
the invariance budget is still affordable. Below that ($k\!=\!3$),
augmentation works fine but PE is too thin for the augmented model to
benefit at large $h$, so \texttt{oap} narrowly wins at $h\!=\!512$. Above
that ($k\!=\!16$), the invariance burden overwhelms the budget and
\texttt{oap}'s deterministic shortcut wins everywhere.

This predicts that with a much larger epoch budget, $k\!=\!16$ would
recover --- not tested here.

\subsection{Test-time averaging $\Delta$ grows with $k$ --- but not enough to matter}

The aug-averaged $\Delta$ is monotone in $k$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{r r r r}
\toprule
$k$  & sign-group size & best $\Delta$ aug at $h\!=\!512$ \\
\midrule
$3$  & $8$             & $+0.0002$ \\
$8$  & $256$           & $+0.0005$ \\
$16$ & $65{,}536$      & $+0.0006$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So averaging \emph{does} matter more as the ambiguity group grows --- but
the absolute size stays in the noise floor. It cannot rescue a
\texttt{random\_augmented} model that has under-fit invariance: at
$k\!=\!16$ where averaging would matter most, it still leaves
\texttt{random\_augmented} $\sim$$+0.004$ AP behind \texttt{oap}.
\emph{Test-time averaging is a free polish, not a substitute for sufficient
training-time invariance.}

\subsection{Canonicalization is the lower-variance default at the extremes of $k$}

Across the three sweeps the seed variance of \texttt{oap} is consistently
smaller than \texttt{random\_augmented}'s --- typically by $2$--$5\times$
at $h\!=\!512$. The implication is the same at every $k$ where
\texttt{oap} wins: it is the safer choice when $3$-seed runs are all
the budget allows. The $k\!=\!8$ result is the exception, not a global
takeaway --- it is the one cell where \texttt{random\_augmented}'s mean
is high enough to overcome its wider variance.

\section{Main conclusions}
%==============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The verdict is non-monotonic in $k$.} Across the $9$ tested
      $(k,h)$ cells, \texttt{random\_augmented} wins $5$ and \texttt{oap}
      wins $4$. The split tracks $k$: \texttt{random\_augmented} sweeps
      at $k\!=\!8$, \texttt{oap} sweeps at $k\!=\!16$, and at $k\!=\!3$
      they share the row.

\item \textbf{$k\!=\!8$ is a sweet spot for random augmentation.} It is the
      only $k$ value where \texttt{random\_augmented} wins at every $h$
      tested. At $k\!=\!3$ the PE is too thin for augmentation to pay off
      at large $h$; at $k\!=\!16$ the invariance burden ($\mathbb{Z}_2^{16}\!=\!65{,}536$
      sign patterns) overwhelms the $200$-epoch budget.

\item \textbf{Test-time ambiguity-group averaging is safe-and-free, not
      transformative.} The $\Delta$ grows with $k$ ($+0.0002$ at
      $k\!=\!3$, $+0.0005$ at $k\!=\!8$, $+0.0008$ at $k\!=\!16$) but
      never enough to flip a verdict. Use it if it's free; do not expect
      it to rescue an under-trained augmented model.

\item \textbf{Practical recipe} for GIN $+$ LapPE on molpcba-like data:
      \emph{$k$-dependent.} Around $k\!=\!8$, train with
      \texttt{random\_augmented} (best at every $h$); add $K\!=\!8$--$16$
      test-time averaging for a tiny free bump. At $k\!=\!3$ at large $h$,
      or $k\!=\!16$ at any $h$, prefer \texttt{oap}. There is no single
      training method that wins across the entire $(k,h)$ grid.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Caveats.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item All numbers are 3-seed averages. Several gaps are within $1$--$2\sigma$
      (notably the $k\!=\!16$/$h\!=\!16$ \texttt{oap} lead of $+0.0048$
      vs.\ \texttt{oap} std $0.0054$).
\item Restricted to GIN, ogbg-molpcba, $k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$,
      $h\!\in\!\{16,128,512\}$. Whether the non-monotonicity continues at
      $k\!=\!32$, with longer epoch budgets at $k\!=\!16$, or on other
      datasets / backbones is not tested here.
\item The $k\!=\!16$ ``random\_augmented under-fits invariance''
      explanation is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Testing it
      directly would require either (a) a longer epoch budget at
      $k\!=\!16$, or (b) explicit instrumentation of how invariant the
      model's predictions are across draws. Neither was done here.
\item \texttt{spielman}, \texttt{maxabs}, \texttt{abs}, \texttt{none} were
      out of scope for these three sweeps. See sibling
      \texttt{results/nadav\_improvements/} for those methods under a
      different (inconsistent-budget) protocol.
\end{itemize}
