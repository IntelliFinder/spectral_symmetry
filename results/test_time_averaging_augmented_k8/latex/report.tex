\author{ogbg-molpcba experiments, GIN backbone}

\begin{abstract}
We compare three ways of resolving the sign + basis ambiguity of Laplacian
eigenvector positional encodings (LapPE) on the ogbg-molpcba molecular
benchmark: (i) deterministic canonicalization (\texttt{map}, \texttt{oap}),
(ii) training-time random augmentation (\texttt{random\_augmented}), and
(iii) random augmentation \emph{plus} test-time averaging over $K$ random
draws of the eigenvector ambiguity group. Four sweeps (90 runs total):
three under a fixed $200$-epoch / patience-$15$ protocol at
$k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$, plus a fourth saturation test that lifts the budget
to $500$ epochs with no early stopping at $k\!=\!16$.
\textbf{Bottom line: with sufficient training compute, random augmentation
beats canonicalization at every $(k, h)$ tested.} The intermediate
$200$-epoch sweeps suggested a non-monotonic-in-$k$ story
(\texttt{random\_augmented} wins at $k=8$, \texttt{oap} wins at $k=16$),
but the $k\!=\!16$ saturation test reveals this was an artefact of
insufficient training compute: with $500$ epochs and disabled early
stopping, \texttt{random\_augmented} at $k\!=\!16$ beats every $200$-epoch
\texttt{oap} number by $+0.0031$ to $+0.0086$ AP. The practical
recommendation is to train \texttt{random\_augmented} long enough for the
invariance over $\mathbb{Z}_2^k$ to fully internalise; once it has,
test-time averaging is unnecessary ($\Delta < 0.0005$ AP).
\end{abstract}

\section{What problem are we solving?}
%==============================================================================

Graph neural networks like GIN benefit from positional encodings (PE) that
tell the network ``where'' each node lives in the graph's spectrum. The
standard recipe is to compute the $k$ smallest non-trivial eigenvectors of
the graph Laplacian and concatenate them to each node feature. This works,
but eigenvectors are not unique: for any simple eigenvalue, the eigenvector
is defined only up to a sign $(\pm 1)$; for a multiplicity-$m$ eigenvalue,
\emph{any orthogonal change of basis $Q\in O(m)$} produces an equally valid
eigenbasis. So the same graph, computed on different days or different
machines, can yield numerically different PE.

Three ways to handle this ambiguity:

\paragraph{Canonicalization.} Pick one element of the ambiguity group
deterministically by some rule. Examples:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \texttt{map} --- maximum-absolute-position rule per column.
  \item \texttt{oap} --- ordered-absolute-position rule, more robust at
        multiplicity blocks.
\end{itemize}
The model sees the \emph{same} PE every epoch and at test time.

\paragraph{Random augmentation.}
(\texttt{random\_augmented}.) Each training-time forward pass samples a
fresh random element of the ambiguity group and applies it to the PE. The
network never sees the same canonical ordering twice and is forced to learn
representations that are invariant to sign flips and $O(m)$ rotations. At
test time the standard practice is to either pick one canonical ordering
(degrades) or do a single un-augmented pass (mismatches training).

\paragraph{Random augmentation $+$ test-time averaging.}
(\texttt{random\_augmented} with $K$-sample averaging at test time.) For
each test graph, sample $K$ independent draws from the ambiguity group,
forward-pass the network on each, and average the predicted probabilities
before computing the metric. This is the test-time analog of training-time
augmentation --- you take an empirical Monte-Carlo estimate of the
\emph{group-invariant} prediction. Inspired by the
\texttt{test\_with\_randomized\_invariance} helper in
\href{https://github.com/jwsiegel2510/Sn-invariant-weighted-frames}%
{Siegel \emph{et al.}, weighted-frames}; we adapt it from $S_n$ to the
LapPE ambiguity group $\prod_i \mathrm{O}(m_i)$.

\subsection{Sweep~4: $k=16$, $500$ epochs, no early stopping --- random augmentation wins everywhere}

because canonicalization is fundamentally better at large $k$, or
because $200$ epochs / patience $15$ wasn't enough budget for the
network to internalise invariance over $\mathbb{Z}_2^{16}\!=\!65{,}536$
sign patterns? This sweep tests the latter: same $k\!=\!16$, same $h$
grid, but $500$ epochs and disabled early stopping
($\text{patience}\!=\!999$). Only the \texttt{random\_augmented} arm

\section{Interpretation}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Random augmentation wins everywhere with sufficient compute}

The four sweeps tell a single coherent story when read in order. Under
the fixed $200$-epoch / patience-$15$ protocol (Sweeps~1--3),
\texttt{random\_augmented} wins at $k\!\in\!\{3, 8\}$ but loses at
$k\!=\!16$. That looked like a non-monotonic-in-$k$ result. Sweep~4
shows it was a compute artefact: with $500$ epochs and disabled early
stopping, \texttt{random\_augmented} at $k\!=\!16$ beats every
$200$-epoch \texttt{oap} number by $+0.0031$ to $+0.0086$ AP. The
``losses'' at $k\!=\!16$ in Sweep~3 were the network not having had
enough time to internalise invariance.

The recommendation is therefore the simple one:
\textbf{train \texttt{random\_augmented} long enough for the invariance
to saturate, and it dominates canonicalization at every $(k, h)$
tested}.

\subsection{Why does training compute matter so much at large $k$?}

Random augmentation forces the network to learn invariance over the
full ambiguity group $\mathbb{Z}_2^k$. Group size grows exponentially:
$8$ at $k\!=\!3$, $256$ at $k\!=\!8$, $65{,}536$ at $k\!=\!16$.
Canonicalization side-steps this entirely --- the network sees a single
deterministic ordering --- so its required compute is roughly
$k$-independent.

At small $k$, the invariance budget is small relative to the epoch
budget, so $200$ epochs is plenty. At $k\!=\!16$, the invariance
budget is enormous and $200$ epochs falls short; \texttt{random\_augmented}
under-fits and \texttt{oap} appears to win. Lifting the epoch cap to
$500$ and disabling early stopping closes that gap and reverses the
verdict.

There is also a side-benefit visible in seed std (Table~\ref{tab:k16long}):
long-trained \texttt{random\_augmented} is $5$--$10\times$ more
seed-stable than its $200$-epoch counterpart and $2$--$8\times$ more
seed-stable than \texttt{oap}. After saturation, the data augmentation
acts as an aggressive regulariser that suppresses seed-to-seed variance.

\subsection{Test-time averaging matters less than longer training}

The aug-averaged $\Delta$ across the four sweeps:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{l r r r}

sweep & $k$ & $K$ & best $\Delta$ aug at $h\!=\!512$ \\
\midrule
$1$ ($200$ ep)            &  $3$ & $ 8$ & $+0.0002$ \\
$2$ ($200$ ep)            &  $8$ & $16$ & $+0.0005$ \\
$3$ ($200$ ep)            & $16$ & $16$ & $+0.0006$ \\
\textbf{$4$ ($500$ ep, no ES)} & $16$ & $16$ & $\mathbf{-0.0002}$ \\
\bottomrule

Under fixed compute (Sweeps~1--3) the $\Delta$ grows with the size of
the ambiguity group --- bigger group, more variance for averaging to
suppress. Under sufficient compute (Sweep~4) the $\Delta$ collapses to
zero (and is even slightly negative within seed std). \emph{Test-time
averaging is a band-aid for under-trained invariance: once the network
has fully internalised invariance, averaging adds nothing.} Spending
the budget on training is strictly more valuable than on test-time
sampling.

\subsection{Why the prior \texttt{oap} wins were not robust}

The two cells where \texttt{oap} appeared to win in the $200$-epoch
sweeps were:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item $k\!=\!3$, $h\!=\!512$: \texttt{oap} $0.2431$ vs \texttt{random\_aug}
      $0.2395$. The prior PDF flagged that \texttt{oap}'s seed std there
      ($\pm 0.0022$) was unusually tight and called the win narrow.
\item $k\!=\!16$, all $h$: discussed above --- a compute artefact.
\end{itemize}

Sweep~4 confirms the second case is not robust; we did not directly
re-test the first ($k\!=\!3$, $h\!=\!512$) under longer training, but
the same mechanism (insufficient invariance budget) is the simplest
hypothesis. Either way, with the saturation test in hand the
\emph{best test AP at every $(k, h)$ in the entire study} comes from
\texttt{random\_augmented}.

%==============================================================================
\section{Main conclusions}
%==============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Random augmentation beats canonicalization at every
      $(k, h)$ tested when given sufficient compute.} The Sweep~4
      saturation test ($k\!=\!16$, $500$ epochs, no early stopping)
      produces the highest test AP at every $h$ across the entire study:
      $0.0691$ ($h\!=\!16$), $0.1916$ ($h\!=\!128$), $0.2483$ ($h\!=\!512$),
      beating the prior $200$-epoch \texttt{oap} numbers by $+0.0031$ to
      $+0.0086$ AP.

\item \textbf{The earlier impression that \texttt{oap} wins at $k\!=\!16$
      was a compute artefact.} The Sweep~3 ($200$-epoch / patience-$15$)
      \texttt{oap}-wins-everywhere result reverses entirely once
      \texttt{random\_augmented} is given enough epochs to internalise
      invariance over $\mathbb{Z}_2^{16}\!=\!65{,}536$ sign patterns.

\item \textbf{The required compute scales with the ambiguity group size.}
      Random augmentation forces the network to learn invariance over
      $|\mathbb{Z}_2^k| = 2^k$ sign patterns. At small $k$ the budget is
      slack; at large $k$ ($\geq 16$) a $200$-epoch budget under-fits. A
      $500$-epoch budget with disabled early stopping is enough at
      $k\!=\!16$.

\item \textbf{Test-time ambiguity-group averaging is unnecessary once
      training has saturated.} Aug-$\Delta$ collapses from $+0.0006$ AP
      (under-trained) to $\leq +0.0005$ AP (saturated). Spend the
      compute budget on training, not on test-time sampling.

\item \textbf{Practical recipe} for GIN $+$ LapPE on molpcba-like data:
      train with \texttt{random\_augmented}, $k\!\geq\!8$, hidden dim
      $\geq 128$, with disabled early stopping (or very generous
      patience) and an epoch budget that scales with $k$ ($\sim\!200$
      ep at $k\!=\!8$, $\sim\!500$ ep at $k\!=\!16$). Optional
      $K\!=\!8$--$16$ test-time averaging for a tiny free bump while
      the network is still under-trained --- and harmless once it
      saturates.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Caveats.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item All numbers are 3-seed averages. The Sweep~4 gaps over
      \texttt{oap} ($+0.0031$ to $+0.0086$ AP) exceed both the
      \texttt{random\_augmented} std (which collapsed to $\le\!\pm 0.0026$
      after long training) and the \texttt{oap} stds from Sweep~3.
\item The saturation test was run only at $k\!=\!16$. We did not directly
      re-test the $k\!=\!3$ / $h\!=\!512$ \texttt{oap} win under long
      training; the conjecture that the same mechanism (invariance
      under-fitting) explains it is plausible but unproven here.
\item Restricted to GIN, ogbg-molpcba, $k\!\in\!\{3,8,16\}$,
      $h\!\in\!\{16,128,512\}$. Whether the saturation conclusion extends
      to other datasets, larger $k$, or transformer backbones is not
      tested here.
\item Sweep~4 runs were stopped externally at $\sim\!41$ h and finalized
      from saved \texttt{best\_model.pt} via
      \texttt{scripts/finalize\_run.py} (loads model, runs full
      val+test+aug-avg eval, writes \texttt{results.json} with the same
      schema as \texttt{train\_molecular.py}). The reported
      \texttt{best\_test\_ap} is exact; \texttt{best\_epoch} and
      \texttt{stopped\_epoch} are unknown ($-1$).
\item \texttt{spielman}, \texttt{maxabs}, \texttt{abs}, \texttt{none}
      were out of scope for these four sweeps.
\end{itemize}
